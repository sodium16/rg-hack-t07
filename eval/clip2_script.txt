Before we look at HTTPS itself, let us define two foundational words. Plaintext is readable, unencrypted data, such as a password or a message before it is protected. Ciphertext is the scrambled, unreadable output produced after an encryption algorithm transforms that plaintext using a secret key. Anyone listening on a public Wi-Fi network can read plaintext if it is sent unprotected, whereas ciphertext looks like random gibberish without the right key.

Now let us talk about symmetric encryption. Symmetric encryption is an encryption method where the exact same secret key is used both to lock, or encrypt, the plaintext and to unlock, or decrypt, the ciphertext. Symmetric encryption is very fast, which makes it ideal for streaming large web pages, videos, and files.

The big problem with symmetric encryption on the open internet is key distribution. If your browser and a bank server need to share the exact same secret key before they can talk safely, how do they agree on that secret key over an insecure network without an attacker intercepting it?

That problem is solved by asymmetric encryption, also known as public key cryptography. Asymmetric encryption uses a mathematically linked pair of keys instead of a single key: a public key that anyone is allowed to see and use to encrypt data, and a private key that the server keeps strictly secret and uses to decrypt that data. Whatever is locked with the public key can only be unlocked with its matching private key.

Now, how do we know a website's public key genuinely belongs to your bank and not to an impostor? That is the job of a certificate authority. A certificate authority is a trusted third-party organization that verifies a website's identity and digitally signs its public key inside a digital certificate. Your browser comes pre-installed with a list of trusted certificate authorities, so when a server presents its certificate, your browser can verify the digital signature automatically.

Once your browser trusts the certificate, it runs a quick setup sequence called the TLS handshake. The TLS handshake is the negotiation step at the start of an HTTPS connection where the browser and server verify the server's certificate and securely agree on a shared session key.

Returning to the TLS handshake: why does HTTPS not just use asymmetric encryption for the entire browsing session? Because asymmetric encryption involves heavy modular arithmetic and is hundreds of times slower than symmetric algorithms.

So HTTPS combines both approaches. During the handshake, the browser uses asymmetric encryption—specifically the server's public key—to safely transmit or agree upon a brand-new, temporary shared key for that single session. Then, as soon as both sides have that shared session key, they switch back to symmetric encryption for the rest of the connection. Remember, symmetric encryption means both sides use that exact same shared secret key to encrypt and decrypt all the actual web traffic—your logins, your form submissions, and the webpage data—at full speed.

Let us summarize what happens every time you see the padlock icon in your browser. Your plaintext is transformed into ciphertext; a certificate authority vouches for the server's public key; the TLS handshake uses asymmetric encryption to agree on a shared secret securely; and symmetric encryption protects the rest of your browsing session quickly and privately.
